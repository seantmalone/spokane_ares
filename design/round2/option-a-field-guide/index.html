Join the team

When phones go quiet, radio still gets the message through.

Licensed amateur radio volunteers supporting Spokane County Emergency Management, the National Weather Service, local hospitals and community events. When normal systems are overloaded or down, our members staff the county’s radio room and communications trailer and pass messages by voice and by radio email.

Fig. 1 Step by step

  1. 1A volunteer writes the message on an The general message form of the Incident Command System. form and calls it in on a handheld radio, from a shelter, an event course or home.
  2. 2The The group’s call sign, honoring Ira “Dean” Bula. repeater on Brownes Mountain hears the signal and passes it across the county, on emergency power.
  3. 3The radio room at the county Emergency operations center. copies the message, logs it and hands it to the people who need it.

Every hop is a trained volunteer, and there are backups: an alternate repeater, simplex radio-to-radio, HF radio and Email by radio..

What the team does

One team of licensed amateur radio (“ham”) operators, ready when phones, power or the internet can’t be counted on, and busy the rest of the year. The team has two sides; you can join one or both.

Spokane County Emergency Management’s own volunteer communications unit.

Field marks

Licensing in plain English

The amateur radio exam works like the written test for a driver’s license, with one big difference: every question in the pool is published in advance.

  • Practice

    Drill the published questions free until they feel familiar.

    Practice on HamStudy(opens hamstudy.org)

  • Take a class

    Local classes are typically sponsored by Spokane County Emergency Management, the VHF Club and local ARES.

    License classes(opens ag7qp.com)

  • Pass the exam

    Volunteer examiners give the test, often right after a class. The FCC charges a $35 application fee.

Three ways to visit

None of them asks for a commitment.

  • Listen to the net

    Tuesdays, 8:00 PM, from home

    Tune any radio or scanner that receives the 2-meter band to 147.300 MHz; listening needs no license. Licensed? When net control calls for visitors, give your call sign slowly and phonetically, your name and your location.

  • Come to a training meeting

    Third Thursdays, evenings. Visitors are welcome.

  • Drop into a workshop

    Second Saturdays, 9:00 AM–noon, hands-on. A Winlink workshop follows, 12:30 to 3:30 PM.

Why W7GBU?

Our call sign honors Ira “Dean” Bula, a Spokane ham who mentored a generation of newcomers and started the weekday morning round table known as the 7:30 Bunch. It still meets on the same repeater that carries his call.

“He was a person who made a difference in Spokane by making others feel like they could make a difference themselves.”
