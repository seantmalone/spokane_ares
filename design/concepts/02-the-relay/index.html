Why a county keeps radio volunteers

A three-minute read. Underlined terms are explained in the margin.Tap an underlined term for a plain-English definition. Here for the frequency? Skip to this week’s nets.

Most days, the phones, cell towers and internet in Spokane County do their jobs. Our official plan is written for the other days. It says volunteer radio communicators “may be called upon to supplement existing systems when it is anticipated that those systems may become overloaded or disabled, or when it is necessary to supply communications services where no established links exist.”

That is the whole job in one sentence. We are licensed amateur radio operators, or hamAn amateur radio operator, licensed by the FCC (Federal Communications Commission) to transmit on the amateur bands., who volunteer to provide backup communications when normal systems are overloaded or down. Eastern Washington’s standing order for emergency communicators explains why the county wants us: “Amateur radio can be particularly helpful because it can function even if other modes of communication cannot.”

The team has two sides that train and operate together. ARESAmateur Radio Emergency Service. A national program of the American Radio Relay League (ARRL), open to any licensed ham. is open to any licensed ham. Its members support community events, the National Weather Service and the Red Cross without an emergency being declared.

ACSAuxiliary Communications. The county’s own volunteer radio unit. Members pass a background check. is Spokane County Emergency Management’s own volunteer communications unit. It carries on the county’s RACESRadio Amateur Civil Emergency Service. The FCC provision that lets hams operate for government in emergencies, and the source of our old name, ARES/RACES. program. ACS members have passed a county background check, so the county can activate them to work in emergency operations centers, incident command posts, hospitals and shelters. You can join ARES only, or both.

Most of the work is practice. Every Tuesday at 8 PM, members check in to a directed net on the W7GBUOur call sign and repeater, in memory of Ira “Dean” Bula. 147.300 MHz, +600 kHz offset, 100 Hz tone. repeater, and we take turns serving as net controlThe operator who runs a net (a scheduled on-air meeting), calling stations in order. A Net Manager coaches new net controls.. Visitors are invited to check in too. As our new-member orientation puts it, “An emergency is a poor time to be handing out business cards.”

Between nets, members send WinlinkEmail-style messages sent by radio. We run our own gateway, W7GBU-10. messages every two weeks, written on ICS-213The general message form of the Incident Command System (ICS), the structure agencies use to run an incident. forms. On the second Saturday of each month we hold a hands-on workshop on things like emergency power, mobile installs and cross-band repeating. On the third Thursday we train together at the county’s Emergency Management building.

Then there are the days everyone can see. We provide communications for Bloomsday and the Lilac Torchlight Parade, and members staff the county’s radio room and comm trailer. A hospital coordinator keeps backup radio and computer equipment in place and working at Greater Spokane area hospitals. Many of us are trained SKYWARNThe National Weather Service’s storm-spotter program. You don’t need a radio license for the training. spotters, and we activate nets for the National Weather Service when severe weather arrives.

When it is real, the county activates the team and obtains a state mission number, so volunteers are covered under RCW 38.52Washington’s emergency management law, a chapter of the Revised Code of Washington. A state mission number brings volunteer work under it.. That structure matters to the agencies we serve, and so does the fine print: nobody here becomes a first responder. The Eastern Washington order says it plainly: “ARES is an auxiliary support function.”Who we serveCounty Emergency Management, the National Weather Service, area hospitals, the Red Cross and community events. None of this requires being an expert on day one. You need a Technician licenseThe entry-level amateur license from the FCC. You earn it by passing an exam; the FCC charges a $35 application fee. or higher, and ARRL membership is not required. Members bring their own radios, and there are no specific equipment requirements, though a station should be able to run for at least 72 hours. The ACS task book writes the commitment down: check in to the net at least monthly, come to a meeting or workshop each quarter, and take part in one field operation a year.Not licensed yet?Anyone with a scanner can listen to Tuesday’s net while you study. Your next step

Three ways in, sized from one Tuesday evening to a standing commitment. Start wherever you’re comfortable.

  1. One evening, from home

    Listen on Tuesday

    Tune to 147.300 MHz at 8:00 PM. If you’re licensed, check in when net control calls for visitors.

    Add the Tuesday net to my calendar

  2. One evening or Saturday morning, in person

    Come to a meeting

    Third Thursday training meetings and Second Saturday workshops are held at Spokane County Emergency Management, 1121 W. Gardner Ave. New Member Orientation is usually the fourth Tuesday.

    See upcoming dates

  3. An ongoing role

    Register for ARES

    Fill in the Spokane County ARES registration form, join our Groups.io list, and start checking in to the net. ARRL membership is not required.

    Join the team
    Want the county to be able to call on you? Add ACS.
    1. Submit the county’s Volunteer Emergency Worker Application.
    2. Pass a background check. The county assigns you a volunteer number.
    3. Attend New Member Orientation, usually the fourth Tuesday.
    4. Complete FEMA’s free online courses IS-100 and IS-700, and work through the ACS task book.
    5. After a six-month activity period, receive your county volunteer ID card.

Not licensed yet? Here’s the path.

You can start before you have a call sign. Listening needs no license.

  1. Listen and look around

    Listen to the Tuesday net, or come to a meeting or workshop as a guest. You can also take SKYWARN spotter training from the National Weather Service without a license.

  2. Take a license class

    Classes run through the year, sponsored by Spokane County Emergency Management, the Inland Empire VHF Radio Amateurs and local ARES, and are typically taught by AD7FO. [Confirm current class schedule]

  3. Pass the Technician exam

    Volunteer examiners hold local test sessions, and you can also test online through GLAARG on HamStudy. The FCC charges a $35 application fee.

  4. Register for ARES

    With your call sign in hand, start at the third step above.
