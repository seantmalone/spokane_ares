Twelve homepage concepts, ranked

These are working homepage mock-ups for a full redesign of spokares.org. Each one tries a different idea for getting a visitor to learn more and then sign up, while staying professional and easy to use. They ignore the current site's look and structure. Four judges reviewed every concept, each through a different lens. Click any desktop screenshot to open the live concept in a new tab.

Scoring: each judge rated every concept from 1 to 10 on six criteria (professional, engaging, ease of use, makes you want to learn more, makes you want to sign up, and overall). Each criterion score shown here is the mean of the four judges, rounded to one decimal. The composite is the mean of the six criterion means, and the ranking follows it.

Field Guide

Most people have no mental picture of emergency radio. Friendly technical illustrations and a two-minute 'Is this for me?' quiz give them one, then route each person, especially the unlicensed, to the right first step.

Judges' notes

Prospective volunteer 9/10 overall

Strengths

The hero illustration (Fig. 1) shows in one picture how a message still gets through when phones are down: a volunteer with a handheld, the W7GBU repeater, then the county EOC. A newcomer who has never pictured emergency radio can see it. A three-panel column beside the hero gives three ways in at once: the quiz, the Tuesday net (8:00 PM, 147.300, visitors welcome) and 'Not licensed yet?'. The 5-question 'Is this for me?' quiz works; I clicked through it. Each answer gets a sketch, the trail markers fill in, and it sends you to one route (license, ARES, ACS or SKYWARN) with 'email me my path'. 'Licensing in plain English' (the exam is like a driver's written test, every question is published, where to test, $35 fee) serves the unlicensed resident best of all 12 concepts. 'The trail to joining' (0-3) says there are no dues and that members provide their own radios. Illustrations of the work are mixed with real member photos, there is a Members' shelf of forms for current members, and the page closes with the W7GBU story. The mobile layout holds up.

Weaknesses

The rounded, friendly illustrations and handwritten captions lean edtech or kid-friendly, so served agencies may find it less authoritative than County Standard or Coverage. The quiz appears twice (the hero card and the full section), and many sections compete for attention. There are fewer real faces than in In the Field or Lines Down. The hero illustration is cropped on mobile. Tone is the risk: the page could read as a hobby explainer rather than a county emergency unit.

Served-agency partner 7/10 overall

Strengths

'Fig. 1: How a message gets through when phones are down' shows volunteer, then repeater, then county EOC. It is the fastest plain-language capability explanation in the set and one I'd happily show a commissioner. The 'Is this for me?' quiz and 'Licensing in plain English' (driver's-test analogy, local exam sites, $35 fee) are excellent at routing the unlicensed. The illustrated service tiles (county comm trailer, hospitals, SKYWARN, events) are clear and inclusive. It is polished, with no visible placeholders.

Weaknesses

The rounded cards, handwritten captions and cartoon figures lean consumer-app or children's museum. It is friendly but slightly soft for a unit that works inside EOCs, and gravity and a sense of trained professionals are missing. There are few real photos, so it proves less that the team actually exists. There is no dedicated agency contact path.

UX and maintainability 7/10 overall

Strengths

The best concept for the unlicensed audience. The hero diagram (Fig. 1: handheld, W7GBU repeater, county EOC) gives an instant mental model of what emergency radio is. The side rail stacks three clear doors: 'Is this for me?' quiz, a Tuesday net card with the frequency, and 'Not licensed yet?'. The five-question quiz with trail-blaze progress routes each person to one next step. 'Licensing in plain English' (the driver's-test analogy, the $35 fee, real exam locations) removes the biggest barrier. The 'Field marks' bullets for ARES vs ACS are a charming, useful device. On mobile, the diagram's labels thoughtfully move into a numbered list under the figure. Lexend body text is readable, and CTAs are big, rounded and obvious.

Weaknesses

Custom illustrations (about 32 KB of inline SVG) are hard for volunteers to extend or update, since every new topic needs a new drawing. Caveat handwriting annotations are hard to read at small sizes and for low-vision users. The pastel, rounded, playful tone may undercut seriousness for served agencies judging credibility. Member tasks are thin: the net card is there, but forms, scripts and roster are not surfaced on the homepage. The quiz logic is one more piece of JS to maintain, and a gated quiz can feel like friction for people who already know they want to join.

Art director 8/10 overall

Strengths

The best concept for unlicensed newcomers. The bento-style hero pairs 'When phones go quiet, radio still gets the message through' with an illustrated Fig. 1 (volunteer, repeater on the ridge, county EOC) that explains the system in five seconds. The illustration set is consistent and well drawn: comm trailer, hospital, SKYWARN storm, and a car-versus-handheld 'driver's test' licensing analogy. The 'Is this for me?' quiz fills in trail-blaze progress markers as you answer and routes each person to one clear first step. The trail-to-joining signposts, the Tuesday net card and the green Members' shelf are clear and easy to scan.

Weaknesses

Caveat handwriting, trail signposts and 'Field marks' lean toward edtech or scout camp. The whimsy lowers the gravitas that served agencies and experienced hams expect from an emergency unit. The flat-vector look is common (Notion/Headspace territory), not ownable. Real photos shrink to small taped polaroids, so the actual team is nearly invisible. Four competing cards in the hero crowd the first screen.

When the Lines Go Down

Most people never picture the failure. Walk them through one plausible bad night, an ice storm that takes out power and cell service, and show step by step how a message still gets from a shelter to the EOC by radio. Then reveal that the people who make it work practice every Tuesday, and the reader could be one of them.

Prospective volunteer 8/10 overall

Strengths

It has the strongest emotional hook: 'It's 2 AM. The power's out, and so is cell service.' The scroll-driven diagram works; I checked it at several scroll positions. A red path carries an ICS-213 ('Resident needs oxygen') from the shelter radio operator to net control on W7GBU, the EOC radio room and the hospital net, and each hop is named as a volunteer role. This makes 'what would I actually do' clearer than any other concept. It then turns to 'Radio still works. Because people practice.' and 'Most of the year looks like this' (Bloomsday, SKYWARN, hospitals, Winlink), which balances the drama. 'Could you be the one on the radio?' gives a 1-4 path, a registration button, a not-licensed route and a 'Listen this Tuesday' panel. A 'Skip the story' link serves members. The serif type is handsome and the sunrise colour shift is well done.

Weaknesses

The join path starts about 6,000px down, so anyone who does not scroll the whole story never reaches the ask. The page has long, empty dark stretches between story steps. The crisis framing could feel alarmist to served agencies, although it is labelled 'A scenario'. Net details for members live in the footer. The story depends on scroll animation, which has to degrade well.

Served-agency partner 8/10 overall

Strengths

The best demonstration of value in ICS terms. A warming-shelter ICS-213 to EOC Logistics goes out over W7GBU and is logged on an ICS-309 in the county radio room, then relayed to the hospital net, all clearly badged 'A scenario. Not a real event.' Most sections explain why agencies need this, and it is the page I'd send to commissioners. 'When the county calls' covers the alert ladder, with a clear 'Spokane County Emergency Management activates the team' statement and marks noted as pending permission. Elegant serif typography, restrained.

Weaknesses

The fear-first opening ('It's 2 AM. The power's out, and so is cell service.') needs a PIO check. Shared out of context, a screenshot could circulate as a real alert, and the scenario must match county shelter and EOC plans. The long scroll with large dark gaps makes members click 'Skip the story' every visit. The murky night-sky hero photo is low-information. Sign-up comes only at the end of the story.

UX and maintainability 6/10 overall

Strengths

The most persuasive 'why'. A plausible ice-storm scenario at 2 AM follows one ICS-213 message about a resident on home oxygen from a shelter, through net control on W7GBU, to the EOC radio room and on to the hospital net. A sticky diagram draws each hop with numbered roles, so readers understand how the system works and see themselves in a role. The Crimson Pro headlines are dignified and the tone is serious, not alarmist. It is clearly labeled 'A scenario', has a 'Skip the story' link, and has a reduced-motion variant. On mobile, the sticky figure plus text works. Once built, the story content is mostly static.

Weaknesses

Slow to the point. The full page shows huge empty dark gaps between steps (min-height about 92vh each), so reaching the CTA takes a lot of scrolling. Member tasks are buried under 'Member tools' with nothing on the homepage. The page is long and dark end to end. On mobile, the sticky figure takes about half the viewport while you read. Scrollytelling (sticky positioning plus step triggers) is brittle to edit for a non-developer, and changing the story means redrawing the SVG path states. Returning members and agencies get no quick facts at the top.

Art director 9/10 overall

Strengths

The best-directed piece in the set. It opens with 'It's 2 AM. The power's out, and so is cell service.' set in large Crimson Pro over a dusk sky, which reads like a film title card. A sticky SVG panel draws the message's route in red: shelter, then the W7GBU repeater, then the County EOC, then the hospital. An ICS-213 card appears in the story, so newcomers see the mechanism and agencies see real procedure (ICS-213, ICS-309, the hospital net). Its strongest moment is the scroll from night into a peach dawn, where 'By morning, the message has been handled.' leads to 'Radio still works. Because people practice.' That turns the story straight into the Tuesday-net pitch. It stays responsible for an emergency-services group: a 'A scenario' tag appears throughout, and a 'Skip the story' link serves members. The mobile story frames hold up.

Weaknesses

The page is long (about 10.5k px), and in a static or full-page capture the story leaves big empty stretches of dark background, because it depends on the sticky graphic. Leading with a failure scenario could read as alarmist if the copy isn't kept this measured. After the dawn section, the practical half (steps, alert levels, who we serve) falls back to a looser, more generic layout, and the energy drops. Real photography is scarce: two field photos appear only after the story. The SVG landscape illustration is serviceable, not rich.

Judges' notes

Prospective volunteer 8/10 overall

Strengths

It makes the lowest-friction ask of the set: 'Come say hello on Tuesday night', with 'Plan your first visit' as the main button and 'Join the team' second. A 'This week' card shows the net, net control, simplex status and the next three events. 'Three ways to visit' uses tabs (Listen, Meeting, Workshop) with a date picker. It walks through the net minute by minute (7:55 tune in, officials, members by suffix, 'Visitors: licensed? This is your moment. Not licensed? Keep listening') and says how long it takes, where it is, what to bring and whether you need a license. It also offers Add to calendar and an optional 'Tell us you're coming' RSVP. A newcomer knows exactly what the first evening feels like. Other strong parts: 'Questions newcomers ask', 'Your first month after joining', real photos of members, and quick links for current members.

Weaknesses

The peach 'This week' card and pill buttons look a bit dated and consumer-grade, and the visual identity is the least distinctive of the strong concepts. 'Verify' and 'to confirm' placeholder chips clutter the most important card. The stakes ('when normal systems are overloaded or down') are only told in text, never shown, so the page is welcoming but low on drama. It is long, about 10,800px.

Served-agency partner 6/10 overall

Strengths

Warm, low-pressure entry: 'Come say hello on Tuesday night', a Plan-your-first-visit tab set, a step-by-step net timeline and an optional RSVP form. The 'Who we serve' strip puts the DEM seal first as primary served agency. The 'Training with partners this fall' row (WSDOT exercise, SET, ShakeOut, SHARES, State COMMEX) is great credibility evidence for a partner. There is a clear note that the backup repeater is shared under agreement with IEVHF.

Weaknesses

The hero is about socializing, not capability. An emergency manager lands on a hobby-club greeting and a headset photo. The salmon/peach 'This week' card is off-brand and looks unfinished next to the navy. There are more visible review chips than in any other concept ('frequency: verify', 'time: verify', 'to confirm'), and a slashed-zero font in the frequency reads oddly. The 'Condition Green: normal' badge sits in the hero, where the public could read it as county status.

UX and maintainability 9/10 overall

Strengths

Best balance in the set. The hero 'This week' card works for every audience at once: net time, frequency, offset and tone, net control, the next three events, and member quick links (net script, roster, ICS forms, Groups.io). A member finds this week's net or a form in about one second, and a newcomer sees an active unit. The primary CTA 'Plan your first visit' is a low-commitment yes, and 'Join the team' stays one click away in the header. 'Three ways to visit' (Listen, Meeting, Workshop) is excellent UX: a date picker, a timestamped walk-through of what happens on the net, an at-a-glance box (how long, where, what to bring, license needed?), add-to-calendar and an optional RSVP. The body font is Atkinson Hyperlegible, a real accessibility choice, and text contrast is high throughout. SCDEM is named in the hero and shown with its logo under 'Who we serve', which agency partners will want. The mobile order is right: headline, two full-width CTAs, then the This-week card with member links. A skip link, aria-expanded menu and aria-live regions are in place.

Weaknesses

The weekly data is the maintenance risk: net control, simplex weeks, per-date visit variants and 'verify' chips all need hand edits every week. It needs one data file (JSON or a PHP include) or a Groups.io calendar feed, or it goes stale fast. The peach This-week panel reads slightly soft and off-brand next to the navy. The folder-tab visit widget carries about 230 lines of JS. The RSVP form needs a PHP mail handler and spam protection. 'Plan your first visit' and 'Join the team' compete at equal visual weight in the hero. The page is long, and the ARES/ACS explainer sits below the fold.

Art director 8/10 overall

Strengths

The most humane conversion design. 'Come say hello on Tuesday night.' in tight Bricolage Grotesque, with Atkinson Hyperlegible body text, sets a warm, low-stakes tone. The photo of a member at the mic, overlapped by a peach 'This week' card, is a nice editorial touch. 'Three ways to visit' is the standout module: folder tabs for Listen, Meeting and Workshop, a date picker, a minute-by-minute run of show for the net, an 'At a glance' box and an RSVP form. It removes first-visit anxiety better than anything else here. 'Questions newcomers ask' and the 'Your first month after joining' calendar make membership concrete. It ends with a drawn locator map and tidy member quick links.

Weaknesses

The hero is a fairly standard two-column layout, less iconic than the top three. The peach, navy and red palette feels friendly but a bit community-center, and short on authority. At about 10.8k px it is long, with some repetition (several 'Not licensed' blocks, two 'Who we serve' passes). 'Plan your first visit' as the primary CTA may delay actual registrations. The photo strip ('Scenes from the team') is small and underused.

Prospective volunteer 7/10 overall

Strengths

It is the most polished and credible-looking page. The topographic map hero ('Your county. Your neighbors. On the air.') with toggleable layers shows agencies the team's footprint and makes the site feel local. 'What happens at each pin' ties places to the role each one needs. An isometric diagram of the ARES, ACS and County layers explains the two sides well. 'Your route onto the team' (0-3) sits next to a not-licensed list and a short 'Tell us you're interested' form whose radio buttons ask where you are on the route. There is also a calendar strip, a Tuesday listening panel, a members' net-control grid and a clear 'Who we serve'.

Weaknesses

Above the fold there are places and pins, not people, so it proves reach more than belonging. The coverage shading is illustrative, which makes the 'Is my home in reach?' ZIP lookup a bit of a gimmick. Repeater reach means little to an unlicensed visitor. The map interaction is fiddly on a phone, and the map will be costly to build and keep accurate.

Served-agency partner 8/10 overall

Strengths

The cartographic hero looks like a county GIS product and instantly shows the operational footprint across Spokane County. It includes the DEM building, NWS and event routes, with repeater reach shown as illustrative. OPSEC is handled thoughtfully: hospitals appear only as a hatched general area, staging and deployment sites are 'members only', and a note says 'sensitive sites are never shown publicly'. The layered ACS/ARES/County diagram and 'What happens at each pin: role it needs' explain capability clearly. It is one of the most credible concepts for partners.

Weaknesses

'Is my home in reach?' is meant for recruits, but residents could misread it as ARES serving individual households in an emergency. The wording needs care. The map must be accurate and well maintained, or it quickly looks amateur. On mobile the map pushes the headline below the fold. The join CTA competes with map interaction. There is no dedicated agency contact.

UX and maintainability 4/10 overall

Strengths

Genuinely local and memorable: a topo-style Spokane County map with layer toggles, pins for where we meet, hospitals (shown only as a general area), public events, NWS and field stations, and a side panel for each pin with 'Role it needs' plus Join/Listen CTAs. It shows agencies the footprint. It is careful about sensitive sites: staging is 'members only', and hospitals are hatched regions, not pins. A top strip gives net info. 'What happens at each pin' repeats the content as accessible static text, a good fallback.

Weaknesses

The hardest concept to maintain. The terrain and map are generated procedurally in about 580 lines of JS, and pins sit at hand-set coordinates, so no volunteer could add a pin or change a layer safely. 'Illustrative' and 'approximate' disclaimers throughout weaken the authority the map is meant to project. 'Is my home in reach?' implies a repeater-coverage prediction the group can't stand behind. On mobile, the map takes the whole first screen and pushes the headline and CTAs below it, and small pin targets plus zoom controls are awkward on touch and keyboard. Members get little beyond the top strip.

Art director 9/10 overall

Strengths

The most distinctive and locally grounded concept. The hero is a hillshade topographic map of Spokane County, rendered well: contours, rivers, Deer Park, Cheney and Mount Spokane labels, a scale bar, and coordinates. It carries red pins for where the team meets, hospitals, public events, NWS and field stations, and each pin opens a side panel with a photo, what volunteers do there, the role it needs, and Join and Listen buttons. That turns curiosity into roles directly. The headline, 'Your county. Your neighbors. On the air.', set in heavy Overpass (a typeface with a road-sign feel), suits the idea. The isometric County/ARES/ACS layer diagram is the clearest explanation of the two-program structure anywhere in the set. 'Is my home in reach?' ZIP lookup, the on-page interest form and the calendar strip all work. It is also the strongest credibility signal for agency partners.

Weaknesses

On mobile the map fills the first screen before the headline and CTAs. On desktop the headline card and layers panel cover about a third of the map. Building and maintaining it is heavy: about 580 lines of JS plus geography and pin data. Many 'illustrative' and 'example' labels show that the content isn't real yet. Human photography only appears inside pin panels, and below the map the page becomes a competent but conventional navy-and-red site.

Prospective volunteer 8/10 overall

Strengths

It has the most motivating recruiting voice ('MAKE A DIFFERENCE WITH A RADIO IN YOUR HAND.') and the most honest commitment block. 'Here's what it takes' sets out 1 net check-in a month, 1 meeting a quarter, 1 field operation a year and a 72-hour go-kit. 'Pick your post' lists 7 roles (net control, field and shelter operator, comm room and EOC, hospital, Winlink, public events, SKYWARN), each with an eligibility tag (Any member, ACS, No license needed). That directly answers 'what would I do'. 'Four steps to your county volunteer ID' has 'I have this' checkboxes and a sample ID card, so joining becomes a concrete goal. 'Not licensed yet? The task book lists FEMA IS-100/200/700/800, NIMS, ARRL EmComm and HIPAA, which is visible NIMS alignment an emergency manager immediately trusts. Strengths

The best member portal. It has sidebar navigation (This week, Nets, Calendar, Forms, Task books, Go-kits, Status codes), search, a Condition Green indicator, a net-control roster with 'Take this slot', a task-book progress ring, a Winlink assignment card, a document library, an offline-copy toggle, and a persistent 'Not a member yet?' recruiting column with a photo and steps. Recommendation

No judge's top pick matched another's, and the top three composites sit within 0.4 points of each other. The strongest path is to carry three directions into round two and graft in the best pieces from the rest. The full reasoning is in REVIEW.md.

1. Field Guide as the newcomer front door

Keep Fig. 1 (handheld, repeater, EOC), the “Is this for me?” quiz and “Licensing in plain English”. Fix the tone for agencies: drop the Caveat handwriting, thin out the four competing hero cards, show real members at a useful size, and add an agency door.

2. Open Door as the structure and usability base

Keep the “This week” card, “Three ways to visit”, member quick links and Atkinson Hyperlegible. Feed every weekly item from one data file, restyle the peach card, add a line of capability to the hero for agencies, and stop the two hero buttons competing at equal weight.

3. When the Lines Go Down as the “How it works” story

All four judges wanted its message-path diagram in the final site. Use it as a homepage section and a full “How it works” page, not as the whole homepage, and get a PIO check on the scenario copy.

Worth grafting in from the other concepts

  • County Standard: the ARES vs ACS comparison table, the “I represent an agency” door with “Contact the emergency coordinator”, and the footer organised by audience.
  • Hi-Vis: “Here's what it takes” (1 net a month, 1 meeting a quarter, 1 field operation a year, 72-hour go-kit) and “Pick your post” with eligibility tags.
  • Plain Signal: the 0 to 3 join path with “How long” and “You need”, plus the shareable ?from= link.
  • Coverage: a lightweight static county map for “Where we work”, keeping its handling of sensitive sites, and the short interest form. Drop the ZIP lookup.
  • Tuesday, 2000 Hours: the self-computing next-net line, “Copy radio settings”, and an action button on every join step.
  • In the Field: “Seven places you could stand”, as an alternative to Pick your post, once a photo shoot happens.
  • The Relay: tap-to-define glossary terms for acronyms, and the headline “From Bloomsday to blackouts”.
  • Ops Board: move it to /members as the member portal.
  • Confirmed Contact: the personalised W7GBU QSL card as a reward for newly licensed hams, off the homepage.

Fix before any direction ships

  • Remove public “Condition Green” status from headers and heroes (01, 04, 05, 10), or label it as team readiness with an expiry. A stale badge during a real incident would contradict the county.
  • Drive the net, net control, simplex weeks and coming-up list from one data file or calendar feed, so the site cannot quietly go stale.
  • Clear every “verify”, “TBD” and “Photo to commission” chip, and get written permission for the county seal and the ARRL emblem.
  • Schedule a photo shoot. Every concept draws on the same five or six photos, most of them low resolution. Hospital or EOC scenes need releases and an OPSEC review.
