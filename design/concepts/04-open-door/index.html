When normal systems are overloaded or down, we carry the message.

Spokane County ARES-ACS is one team of licensed amateur radio (“ham”) operators who volunteer to provide backup communications for Spokane County Emergency Management and the agencies it serves. We staff the county’s radio room and comm trailer, back up area hospitals, and handle communications for community events.

“Amateur radio can be particularly helpful because it can function even if other modes of communication cannot.”Eastern Washington Standing Order 1b

ARES

Amateur Radio Emergency Service

A national ARRL program open to any licensed ham. ARES members can support community events, the National Weather Service and the Red Cross without an emergency being declared.

ACS

Auxiliary Communications

Spokane County Emergency Management’s own volunteer communications unit, carrying on the county’s RACES program. ACS members pass a county background check, so the county can activate them to work in emergency operations centers, incident command posts, hospitals and shelters.

You can join ARES only, or both. The two sides train and operate together. In the group’s own words: “Even if you don’t or can’t qualify for ACS, our ARES group welcomes you.”

Who we serve

  • Primary served agency

    Spokane County Emergency Management

    Activates the team under a state mission number, runs the ACS background check and issues volunteer ID cards.

    Plan your first visit

    • “Do I need my own radio?”

      Not to visit. To listen from home, any radio or scanner that receives 147.300 MHz will do. Members bring their own gear, but there are no specific equipment requirements, and a Second Saturday workshop is a good place to ask what’s worth buying.

    • “I’m not licensed. Can I still come?”

      Yes. Anyone can listen to the net, and visitors are welcome at meetings and workshops. To check in on the air or to join, you’ll need an FCC Technician license or higher. Local classes and exam sessions can get you there. See the way to a license.

    • “How much time does it take?”

      The current task book asks members to check in to the Tuesday net at least once a month, come to a meeting or workshop once a quarter, and take part in one field operation a year: an activation, an exercise or a public service event. Weekly check-ins and monthly meetings are encouraged. confirm with leadership

    • “Do I have to be an expert?”

      No. New members have up to about a year to work toward the Radio Operator level. Net control rotates among members, and the Net Manager coaches anyone taking a turn for the first time.

    • “What happens in a real emergency?”

      The county activates the team, and members follow four alert levels:

      1. GreenNormal. Monthly battery check.
      2. YellowSomething might happen. Check go-kits, monitor.
      3. OrangeSomething has happened. Load the car, monitor W7GBU and NWS.
      4. RedDeploy. Check in with net control on W7GBU.

      Members aren’t first responders. ARES is an auxiliary support function, working alongside the agencies that are.

    Not licensed yet? Start here.

    You need an FCC Technician license or higher to transmit and to join. Getting one is a known, local path.

    1. Listen and visit firstListening to the net needs no license, and visitors are welcome at meetings and workshops.
    2. Take a license classClasses run periodically through the year, typically led by AD7FO and sponsored by Spokane County Emergency Management, the VHF Club and local ARES. verify schedule
    3. Take the examWashington Digital Radio Enthusiasts test on the third Wednesday at 6 PM, 1027 E Marietta Ave, or test online through GLAARG on HamStudy. Volunteer examiners charge nothing; the FCC charges a $35 application fee. verify

    Want to help before you’re licensed? SKYWARN spotter training from the National Weather Service needs no license.

    Later, if you want: qualify for ACS

    ACS lets the county call on you and puts you inside emergency operations centers, hospitals and shelters.

    1. Apply to the countySubmit the Volunteer Emergency Worker Application.
    2. Pass a background checkThe county assigns you a volunteer number.
    3. Train toward Radio OperatorYou’re a new member for up to about a year, working through the ACS Task Book.
    4. Receive your county ID cardAfter IS-100, IS-700, the background check and six months of activity.
