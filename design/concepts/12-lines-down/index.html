A scenario: how a message gets from a shelter to a hospital without phones

A scenario Not a real event. It shows how the system is meant to work.

First the power goes. Then the phones.

Ice has been building on the lines since dusk. Around midnight they start to come down.

Cell towers fall back on their batteries, then go quiet. Home internet goes with them. There’s no way to call anyone.

At a warming shelter, someone needs help.

Neighbors without heat have gathered at a shelter. One of them uses oxygen at home, and the concentrator needs power. The shelter manager has to reach the county’s Emergency Operations Center.

A scenario. The message is illustrative.

A volunteer picks up the radio.

A licensed ham assigned to the shelter has a go-kit: a radio, an antenna and batteries that don’t depend on the grid.

They call net control on the W7GBU repeater, 147.300 MHz, and read the message out word for word. Net control keeps the frequency orderly, so every station gets its turn.

ACS members have passed a county background check, so they can work inside shelters, hospitals and the EOC.

The EOC writes it down and passes it on.

In the county’s radio room, another volunteer copies the message and logs it on an ICS-309. The EOC acts on it, and the request goes out on the hospital net, where a hospital team operator is listening.

None of this needs a cell tower, or the internet.

By morning, the message has been handled.

Radio still works. Because people practice.

None of that night depends on luck. It depends on volunteers who have practiced on ordinary Tuesdays, so that a real night feels familiar.

“Amateur radio can be particularly helpful because it can function even if other modes of communication cannot.”

Who carries the message

Spokane County ARES-ACS is one team of licensed amateur radio (“ham”) operators who volunteer to provide backup communications when normal systems are overloaded or down.

ARESAmateur Radio Emergency Service

A national ARRL program, open to any licensed ham. ARES members support community events, the National Weather Service and the Red Cross, without an emergency being declared.

ACSAuxiliary Communications

Spokane County Emergency Management’s own volunteer communications unit. It carries on the county’s RACES program (Radio Amateur Civil Emergency Service), the FCC provision that lets hams operate for government during emergencies.

ACS members have passed a county background check, so the county can activate them to work in emergency operations centers, incident command posts, hospitals and shelters.

Join ARES only, or both. The two sides train and operate together.

Even [if] you don’t or can’t qualify for ACS, our ARES group welcomes you.

Most of the year looks like this

The same skills that carry a message on a bad night are used all year, often in daylight.

Public events
Primary or backup communications for Bloomsday, the Lilac Torchlight Parade, fun runs and bike races.
Hospitals
Backup radio and computer equipment kept ready at Greater Spokane area hospitals, and a hospital net.
SKYWARN
Weather spotting for the National Weather Service in Spokane. Encouraged, not required.
Digital messaging
The W7GBU-10 Winlink gateway, and regular Winlink assignments sent on ICS-213 forms.
County radio room
Staffing the county’s Radio Communications room and comm trailer when activated.
Exercises and training
The Simulated Emergency Test, state exercises, and monthly meetings and workshops.

About the scenario above: ARES-ACS is an auxiliary support function. Volunteers back up the county’s own systems when asked; they are not first responders.

Could you be the one on the radio?

The repeater in this story carries the call sign W7GBU, a memorial call for Ira “Dean” Bula.

“He was a person who made a difference in Spokane by making others feel like they could make a difference themselves.”

You need an amateur radio license and a willingness to practice. ARRL membership isn’t required, and there are no specific equipment requirements. Meetings, workshops and a coached net help with the rest.

Not licensed yet?

You can start before you have a license. Here’s the usual order.

  1. Listen to the Tuesday netAny radio or scanner that receives the 2-meter band can tune to 147.300 MHz at 8 PM.
  2. Take a license classClasses run periodically through the year, sponsored by Spokane County Emergency Management, the VHF Club and local ARES. Ask about the next class
  3. Pass the Technician examAt a local volunteer examiner session, or online through GLAARG on HamStudy. The FCC charges a $35 application fee.
  4. Train as a weather spotter (no license needed)SKYWARN spotter training from the National Weather Service in Spokane, online or in person.

When the county calls

Members follow four alert levels, from a quiet month to a full deployment. Green is normal.

  1. Condition GreenInactive

    Normal conditions. Do a monthly battery check.

  2. Condition YellowAlert

    Something might happen. Check go-kits and monitor frequencies.

  3. Condition OrangeStand by

    Something has happened. Load the car and monitor NWS WXL86 (162.400) and W7GBU (147.300).

  4. Condition RedDeploy

    Activation text sent and full nets start. Check in with net control on W7GBU (147.300).

After the event: members are released, the level returns to Green, and an action review is held within 72 hours.
